Option prices aren't just numbers — they encode deep mathematical structure. Violations of that structure are money lying on the pavement.

**What it is:** Totally Positive of Order 2 (TP₂) is a property of call option prices viewed as a function $C(K, T)$ of strike $K$ and maturity $T$. A function $f(x, y)$ is TP₂ if the determinant of every $2 \times 2$ submatrix is non-negative:

$$
\det\begin{pmatrix} f(x_1, y_1) & f(x_1, y_2) \\ f(x_2, y_1) & f(x_2, y_2) \end{pmatrix} \ge 0, \quad \text{for all } x_1 < x_2, \; y_1 < y_2
$$

For call options with strikes $K_1 < K_2$ and maturities $T_1 < T_2$, TP₂ means:

$$
\frac{C(K_2, T_1)}{C(K_1, T_1)} \le \frac{C(K_2, T_2)}{C(K_1, T_2)}
$$

In words: the ratio of a higher-strike call to a lower-strike call *increases* with time to expiry. This strengthens the standard no-calendar-spread-arbitrage condition and holds in Black-Scholes, Heston, and many other models. Glasserman, Li & Pirjol (2025) show it does **not** always hold in market data — and when it breaks, the mispricing is exploitable.

**Why it matters / How it is used:** TP₂ violations signal that the relative expensiveness of high-strike vs low-strike calls is inverted across maturities — a structural inconsistency no rational model should produce. The trading strategy is a calendar spread: go long the relatively cheap pair and short the relatively expensive one. In their paper, the authors report:

- **~4% monthly returns** before transaction costs
- **Sharpe ratios above 2** after accounting for bid-ask spreads
- **>95% hit rates** on individual violation trades
- Violations typically correct within **2-3 days**

An independent replication by Zhou (2024) at Bloomberg using intraday data confirmed the results hold even after realistic transaction costs.

**Quick example:** Suppose SPX options trade at these prices (hypothetical):

| Strike | 30-day Call | 60-day Call |
|--------|-------------|-------------|
| 5500   | $120        | $175        |
| 5600   | $85         | $130        |

Check TP₂:
- Ratio at 30d: $85/$120 = 0.708
- Ratio at 60d: $130/$175 = 0.743
- 0.708 ≤ 0.743 ✓ — TP₂ holds.

A violation would look like: 30-day ratio = 0.75, 60-day ratio = 0.70 (the ratio *decreased* with maturity — meaning high-strike calls are relatively cheaper at longer maturities, which should not happen). The trade: buy the 60-day 5600/5500 call spread and sell the 30-day 5600/5500 call spread.

**The mathematics:** The TP₂ condition for calls can be rewritten in terms of implied volatilities. For a given pair of strikes $(K_1, K_2)$ and maturities $(T_1, T_2)$, a violation occurs when:

$$
\frac{C(K_2, T_2)}{C(K_1, T_2)} < \frac{C(K_2, T_1)}{C(K_1, T_1)}
$$

Equivalently, in log-space with $\kappa = \log(K/F)$:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial T} \left[ \frac{\partial \log C}{\partial \kappa} \right] < 0
$$

The paper also defines an **RR₂** (reverse ratio) property for puts and finds analogous trading opportunities.
